The first ten people in a company aren't employees. They're co-founders without
co-founder equity. They will determine the speed, the culture and, frankly, the
company's odds more than the product strategy will. Here are the mistakes I made
myself — and now see in every second portfolio team.

## Mistakes 1–4: who we hire

**1. Hiring by resume brands.** Someone from Google is used to resources,
processes and a brand that opens doors. A five-person startup has none of that.
Don't ask "where did you work" — ask "what did you do when nobody was watching."

**2. Hiring "no weak spots."** A candidate without weaknesses is usually a
candidate without strengths. Early teams are built from spikes: one sells
brilliantly but can't do process; another codes like a god but can't stand
meetings. You cover the early employees' weaknesses — that's what you're for.

**3. Hiring "for scale."** "He'll be a great VP Engineering once we're 50."
When you're 50, you'll hire a VP Engineering from the market. Right now you need
someone who solves this quarter's problem.

**4. Ignoring the ability to sell.** In the first ten, everyone sells: the
engineer sells the company to candidates, the designer sells decisions to the
team, you sell everything to everyone. A person who "just does their job" is a
luxury of a 200-person company.

## Mistakes 5–8: how we hire

**5. Hiring fast because it's on fire.** A bad early hire costs 6–12 months:
first you don't see what's wrong, then you don't act, then you part ways and
search again. "On fire" is cured by cutting scope, not by the first candidate
who shows up.

**6. Interviewing in the abstract.** "Tell me about a conflict on your team"
tests storytelling. Give a real task from your company — paid, if it takes more
than a couple of hours.

**7. Skipping back-channel reference checks.** Official references are curated.
Find people through your network whom the candidate didn't name — and ask:
"Would you hire them again? What role would you never give them?"

**8. Selling only the dream.** If you sell a candidate only the mission and the
options, you get someone with unrealistic expectations. Sell honestly: "it will
be hard, the salary is below market, but here's what you'll build and learn."

## Mistakes 9–12: what we do after

**9. No onboarding.** "We're a startup, you'll figure it out" isn't culture —
it's laziness. Write two pages: how we make decisions, how we communicate,
what's not done here. It pays back on every next hire.

**10. Delaying the parting.** In a small team everyone sees who isn't pulling
their weight. Every month of hesitation reads to the team as "so that's okay
here."

**11. Copying big-company culture.** Values on the wall, regimented 1:1s,
performance reviews — all of that makes sense at 50+ people. At 10, one thing
makes sense: the founder talks to everyone, personally and regularly.

**12. Treating hiring as an HR task.** Early on, hiring is the founder's job.
You close each of the first twenty people personally. If you "don't have time
for hiring" — you don't have time to build a company.
